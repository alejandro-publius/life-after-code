# Past winners: GitLab, Google Cloud and Anthropic hackathons, January 2025 to October 2026

Part 1 of the research handoff for Alex Velazquez, solo entrant in Life After Code, the GitLab Transcend Hackathon (October 2026; judges from GitLab, Google and Anthropic). Compiled on 2026-10-06 from the research notes in [research/winners/](research/winners/) and the June judging note quoted in [research/gitlab_guide_and_reference.md](research/gitlab_guide_and_reference.md). No new web research was done for this file. Cross-event patterns belong to Part 2 (docs/PATTERNS.md, listed as pending in [STATUS.md](STATUS.md)).

Conventions:

- Quotes keep the source words. Where a source used an em or en dash, it is written as " - " ([DECISIONS.md](DECISIONS.md)).
- "(search excerpt)" marks text taken from a search engine summary of a page that could not be opened. "(unverified)" marks anything that no opened source confirms. "(participant copy)" or "(team copy)" marks rules text that an entrant copied into their own repo.
- "Likely reasons" are the researchers' reading of the evidence, not statements by judges.
- Each event section names the note it comes from. Follow that link for the full evidence behind every line.

## 1. Scope and method

### Scope

- Period: January 1, 2025 to October 6, 2026.
- Included: (1) every hackathon that GitLab hosted, co-hosted or sponsored and that the research found; (2) hackathons sponsored by Google Cloud or Anthropic with an agentic, DevOps or developer-tools theme. Build with DataHub is also covered, because Alex won its Grand Prize, although no Anthropic role in it was found.
- Noted but not counted: events with an off-theme focus (for example the BigQuery AI Hackathon), events where GitLab's or Anthropic's role is unconfirmed, campus events, and events outside the dates. They are listed at the end of section 4.

### How events and winners were found

- Web search, until a limit of 200 searches per turn, shared by all research agents in the session, ran out partway through the work. Each note lists its own searches.
- Sources that could be opened: the gitlab.com REST and GraphQL APIs (groups, repos, issues, merge requests, pipelines, the public AI Catalog through `aiCatalogItems`, and the CI/CD Catalog); cloud.google.com (winners posts, announcements, the "What's new in Google Cloud" logs, site maps); claude.com (winners posts, read through WebFetch, which returns a model-written summary, so a quote can be off by a word); github.com pages through WebFetch and raw.githubusercontent.com; and the source file of Gradio's winners page on GitHub.
- Winners were matched to repos through GitLab's public AI Catalog (each published agent or flow names its source project), README titles in the organisers' workspace groups, links in winners posts and READMEs, and GitHub search.

### What was blocked

devpost.com and every *.devpost.com page, youtube.com and vimeo.com, about.gitlab.com, forum.gitlab.com, contributors.gitlab.com, cerebralvalley.ai, datahub.com, huggingface.co, gradio.app, x.com, linkedin.com, medium.com, dev.to, and most live demo hosts (run.app, vercel.app, fly.dev, gitlab.io). The search budget also ran out. Section 5 has the details and what could fill each gap.

**The brief asked for each winning project's Devpost page, its video (or transcript) and its repo. That bar was not met for any project, because of the blocks.** No winner's Devpost page was opened and no video was watched. Video lengths below come from files in the repos (a video rendered from code, a script, a README claim), never from watching. Live URLs are listed but were not opened.

Other table columns:

- Team: people credited in the winners post or a Devpost excerpt; otherwise the human committers in the repo.
- Autonomy: "suggests" (reports, answers or drafts only); "acts with approval" (a person says yes before the step that matters); "acts up to a human merge" (writes branches, commits, issues or MRs on its own, and a person merges); "acts alone" (takes the consequential action itself).
- Human story: "yes" when the pitch is built around a person or a role in a felt moment, named or not; "no" when it is a problem statement or statistics only.

### 3.1 GitLab AI Hackathon, "You Orchestrate. AI Accelerates." (Feb 9 to Mar 25, 2026)

Notes: [gitlab-ai-hackathon-2026-part1.md](research/winners/gitlab-ai-hackathon-2026-part1.md), [gitlab-ai-hackathon-2026-part2.md](research/winners/gitlab-ai-hackathon-2026-part2.md), cross-checks in [gitlab-transcend-past-editions.md](research/winners/gitlab-transcend-past-editions.md). Evidence: winners from search excerpts of the blocked [winners post](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/gitlab-ai-hackathon-2026-meet-the-winners/) (also blocked: the [forum copy](https://forum.gitlab.com/t/gitlab-ai-hackathon-2026-meet-the-winners/133590)); repos read on gitlab.com. Highest level reachable: B.

- **Host and money.** GitLab, with Google Cloud and Anthropic as co-sponsors; $65,000 in 11 categories and 19 prize slots, on [gitlab.devpost.com](https://gitlab.devpost.com/) (search excerpts). Six amounts are confirmed first-hand by the "Prizes Targeted" table in the [Time-Traveler README](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/34562572/-/blob/main/README.md) and by a participant's [hackathon-context.md](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/35368827/-/blob/main/hackathon-context.md).
- **Size.** "Nearly 7,000 developers" built "600+ AI agents and flows" (search excerpt; Devpost titles show 6,937 to 6,943 participants, for example [devpost.com/bomboclark/challenges](https://devpost.com/bomboclark/challenges)). Read directly: 1,788 workspaces in the [moved group](https://gitlab.com/groups/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon); 506 projects published 1,807 public AI Catalog items (1,042 agents, 765 flows) and 425 published a flow ([GraphQL `aiCatalogItems`](https://gitlab.com/api/graphql)); 1,263 of 1,765 non-empty repos (72%) never changed the one-line template README. Of the 496 custom READMEs, 61% are about security, 40% mention carbon or sustainability, 51% mention Claude or Anthropic, 26% Google Cloud, 13% link a video, and 6% promise full autonomy.
- **Brief**, from the [onboarding issue](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/621485/-/work_items/1) shown to all participants: "Anything that removes friction from the software development lifecycle. Automate security fixes. Streamline code reviews. Generate compliance reports. Flag risks before they become problems." "The best submissions will tell a story: here's the pain, here's how the agent solves it, here's what changes for developers." "Chat alone won't qualify. We want to see agents that react to triggers and take action." "At least one custom public agent or public flow should be created."
- **Judging.** Four criteria as entrants copied them: Technological Implementation, Design, Potential Impact, Quality of the Idea ([3571888](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/3571888), [35092494](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/35092494), [8041942](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/8041942)). Weights and judges not found. Two excerpts disagree on the Green Agent judges: "Kim Buncle and Rajesh Agadi from Google" in one, and "Stacy Cline and Kim Buncle from GitLab's sustainability team" in a [GitLab post of Jul 9, 2026](https://about.gitlab.com/blog/green-devops-carbon-measurement-cicd-pipeline/) (both search excerpts). The Sustainable Design bonus went to "projects with exceptional sustainability practices in their design, from model optimization techniques to energy-efficient architecture choices" (search excerpt).
- **Mechanics and limits.** Each participant got a project with agent and flow templates ([participant template](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/project-templates/participant-template)); a group policy replaced each project's CI with the hackathon CI, which published only `agents/*.yml` and `flows/*.yml` to the catalog ([policy.yml](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/security-policies/-/blob/main/.gitlab/security-policies/policy.yml), [hackathon CI](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/ci/-/blob/main/.gitlab-ci.yml)). Winners wrote about two limits: "All agents and flows running in GitLab's cloud cannot make outbound HTTP requests" ([Gitdefender DEVPOST.md](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/35458144/-/blob/main/DEVPOST.md)) and participants lacked the role "required to register MCP servers" ([Time-Traveler DEVPOST.md](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/34562572/-/blob/main/DEVPOST.md)).
- **Where the notes disagree.** Part 1 lists six $500 honorable mentions from Devpost's prize list and names two of them from search excerpts; part 2's searches found no honorable mention and left $3,000 of the $65,000 unexplained, which equals six $500 mentions. Part 2 did not find the Easiest to Use amount; part 1's Devpost excerpt gives $5,000. The order of winners in the post is a reconstruction. For Gitdefender and RedAgent the post's one-line summary (search excerpt) does not match the repo; the notes treat the repo as the source of truth.

A Python CLI carries 43 test functions and a Pages dashboard; the flow is in [flows/flow.yml](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/35153311/-/blob/main/flows/flow.yml). [MR !10](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-community/community-projects/2026-02-ai-hackathon/35153311/-/merge_requests/10) shows the flow flagging a cache change against "Memory #001", then superseding it after the developer's reply. **Gitdefender (B).** "A reputation scoring engine for open source merge requests" that scores MRs for AI-generated slop. The post's one-liner, "It spots the bug, writes the fix, and opens the code review" (search excerpt), does not match the repo. Facts from the repos, as a whole: all four Technological Implementation and Design winners have successful flow runs and flow-made issues, comments, commits or MRs in their own repos; none of the four Impact and Idea winners has a single flow run. Non-winners ran far more flows (for example [Contributor Compass](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/transcend/39109018) with 481 successful runs, much of it repeated issues). Seven of eight winners show one human committer, and at least three show AI coding tools in their commit metadata. Two winners' authors had entered the February AI Hackathon.

**Sankofa (B).** "Sankofa uses GitLab Orbit's knowledge graph to automatically surface structural code intelligence at three critical moments in the developer lifecycle": Radar (merge request blast radius), Guide (a knowledge brief when an issue is assigned) and Shield (vulnerability propagation, which opens child issues). It ran 38 flow sessions (35 successful) in one evening; Radar's report on [MR !2](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/transcend/34570711/-/merge_requests/2) reads "Risk Level: **HIGH**" and "called by **31 downstream definitions**". Its whole history is one day (June 17). Caveats: one brief lists files that are not in the repo, and some triggers the README promises did not exist in June. README (371 words): Why / Architecture / Requirements / Install / Usage (event-driven; @mention) / Tech Stack / License. Likely reasons: the broadest working platform build among the winners, with run evidence that "an evidence bundle pulled from the actual repo code" could see. Not the same project as the Gemini Live winner called Sankofa (section 3.8).

**Stayed Shipped (B).** "**Merged is not done. Shipped and stayed shipped is done.**" It checks whether each merged MR survived an N-day window, with three evidence classes (revert lineage, bug-fix path overlap, churn) and three author groups (`ai-authored`, `ai-assisted`, `human`). It documents live-API findings that override the public docs ([LIVE-GATES.md](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/transcend/2902648/-/blob/main/docs/LIVE-GATES.md)) and keeps an offline replay of a real capture. Caveats: its first two flow runs wrote into another participant's project (GitLab deleted the artifacts on request, [issue #1](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/transcend/2902648/-/work_items/1)), and its README status table was stale. README (2,170 words): The problem / Run it / Live output (a live run on 2026-06-12 over 128 merged MRs) / Status / Documentation / Repo map / License. Story line: "A merged change that gets reverted four days later is counted as productivity twice". Likely reasons: rigor that shows in code (the largest test suite among the winners) and a question close to October's theme, how much autonomy coding agents earn.

**Carver (B).** "Carver is a GitLab Duo agent that estimates what a legacy migration will cost before you commit to one." "It plans migrations. It does not convert code." Sample output: "CARVER QUOTE - AngularJS 1.x → Angular (latest) / 18 units · 3 layers · ~9 wks @1 dev (~3.5 wks @3) · human ≈$54k · gen ≈$10 (~1.2M tok)". Five skills are picked by intent, golden outputs are committed for each, and the hand-off flow opened [MR !2](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/transcend/13178946/-/merge_requests/2). Caveat: both flow MRs used the committed dependency file, not a live Orbit traversal. README (928 words): What you type / what you get / Carver also acts / Why GitLab / Architecture / Usage / Sample app / Examples / Tests / License. Likely reasons: the clearest interaction design in the set ("One line per unit, with size and risk markers instead of paragraphs. Detail only when you ask for it."), one safe action, and Design was "resolved by hand".

**Marshal (B).** "Declare one org-wide change goal in plain language. marshal discovers the affected fleet via Orbit, reasons out risk and order, lands real merge requests through a pluggable transform engine, and drives every repo to a terminal state - tracked in a live dashboard with human approval gates at every step." The flagship demo moves Java 8 and Spring Boot 2 to Java 21 and Spring Boot 3 with OpenRewrite. [Issue #23](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-ai-hackathon/transcend/10572992/-/work_items/23) shows the gated conversation: analysis, `/approve`, preview, "start phase 1", "start phase 2", `/status`. Caveats: risk scores fell back to heuristics, Wave 1 MRs were never created, and one run opened a campaign issue in another entrant's project. README (2,026 words): The Problem / What marshal Does / Interactive Campaign Flow / Orbit as the Context Layer / Wave-Based Rollout / The Completion Ledger / The Transform Engine / Live Dashboard / State Persistence / Built-In Playbooks / Required Setup / CLI Usage / Local Demo / Repo Layout / "The Before / After" / License. Likely reasons: a gated workflow a person can follow, a dashboard, a narrated demo plan, and a readable trail of real runs.

**CrossCut (B, probable match).** "Run only the tests a change can possibly break. Powered by GitLab Orbit." The CLI reads the diff, walks incoming calls in Orbit, picks the affected test files and writes a child pipeline and an MR comment: "No LLMs, no hallucinations - just deterministic AST graph traversal." Demo claim: "87% compute reduction. Execution drops from 38 minutes to 2 minutes." Caveats: no runnable Duo flow, no pipeline ever ran, the committed output shows a no-op run, and the repo appeared 8 minutes before the deadline, outside the June group, with commits by a June registrant in another user's project. README (562 words): The Story ("The Developer Pain Point", "How Crosscut Fixes It", "What Changes for the Developer?", the three parts of the Devpost story prompt) / Features & Functionality / Architecture / Quick Start / Hackathon Demo Scenario / License. Likely reasons: a direct time and money metric, a deterministic method, a safety override, and a presentation built for judges. Impact was "resolved by hand".

**OrbitWeaver (B).** "An autonomous Duo Agent that executes complex architectural migrations by first querying the GitLab Orbit knowledge graph to map the precise structural "blast radius" of a change", then commits each affected file in dependency order (Kahn's algorithm) and opens an MR with a Mermaid chart. Caveats: its queries are Cypher-style while Orbit's language is JSON, its demo links return 404, its only pipeline failed at lint, and it shows no flow runs. README (2,735 words): Why Not Cursor, Copilot Workspace, or Standard RAG? / The Problem / Tier Requirements / How It Works (stages 1 to 5) / Failure Handling / The Deliverable / Architecture / Repository Structure / Installation / Tips / What's Next / Contributing / License. Likely reasons: an enterprise-sized problem told with a timed human baseline (48 minutes and "3 of 12" files missed by hand, against "12 / 12" in "2 min 47 sec") and a clear contrast with other tools.

**Transcend (B).** "A second reasoning engine built on top of GitLab Orbit's code graph, using the W3C semantic web stack - OWL, SPARQL, RDF - to turn a code intelligence graph into an open, federatable knowledge hub." It answers transitive blast radius and multi-hop cycles, checks the consistency of the graph itself, and joins with Wikidata. Its proof is a benchmark: "the recursive SQL and SPARQL answers agree exactly on every question, every time - 6 for 6". It also publishes a negative result about its own ontology ("**Verified result: no.**"). README (2,929 words): Agent Runtime Requirements / Why / 60-second proof (for judges) / How it works / Installation / Usage / What the benchmark actually verifies / Tests / Repository structure / Status / License. Likely reasons: the most unusual idea in the field and a "non-obvious Orbit use case", the criterion's own words.

**Universal Agent OS (B).** "A consultation-first governance framework for AI coding agents": "interview first, plan before code, track evidence, force explicit validation". Its GitLab demo has "a non-technical founder" turn a plain-English idea ("ParkShare") into issues and an MR in a [demo project](https://gitlab.com/zyganali-group/agent-os-demo-saas). Caveats: the core predates the event (Mar 29, 2026), its "Orbit" client uses the GraphQL API rather than the graph, the flow file is not in v1 shape, and the catalog sync is a mock. README (1,807 words): Demo walkthrough (video chapters) / GitLab Duo Integration & AI Catalog / Honest Status / Why This Exists / Why It Feels Bureaucratic / What The Bureaucracy Buys You / What Ships In This Repository / Product Shape / How It Works / Getting Started / Who This Is For / Who This Is Not For / Roadmap Honesty / Repository Layout / Bottom Line. Likely reasons: a different angle (governing the agents themselves), a human story and a full video, outside both crowded clusters.

**Pipeline Doctor (B).** A Spring Boot service on Cloud Run that listens for GitLab pipeline webhooks. On a failure it pulls the job log, asks Gemini 2.0 Flash for the root cause, finds the three most similar past failures with MongoDB Atlas vector search, posts both on the MR (or emails the author), then asks Gemini for a corrected `.gitlab-ci.yml` and opens an MR labelled `ai-generated` ([GitlabCiFixService.java](https://gitlab.com/vanichitkara18/pipeline-doctor/-/blob/main/src/main/java/com/pipelinedoctor/service/GitlabCiFixService.java)). **Agent Anansi (B).** A FastAPI service on Cloud Run with six Gemini-prompt "agents" (issue triage, code review, security scan, CI optimizer, docs generator and a burnout detector) that return JSON turned into markdown reports ([main.py](https://gitlab.com/prehistoricpancake/agent-anansi/-/blob/main/main.py)). Caveats: the CI jobs post hard-coded samples and never read the real MR, the catalog components were never published, the integration test file is empty, and it was built in four days (Jun 13 to 17, 2025). README: title and tagline ("Building Software. Faster.", the brief's own words), badges ("Production Ready", "GitLab CI/CD Catalog"), Updates (the 3rd-place note), an overview with the Anansi folklore story, "Hackathon Challenge: GitLab Track" with a checklist quoting the brief, The Six AI Agents (five described), Architecture, Quick Start, "Demo Results & Impact" (unsourced numbers such as "95% accuracy"), a sample report, an ASCII diagram, API endpoints, License and Team, a closing quote. Likely reasons for third place: breadth, a memorable name and story, and a README mapped point by point to the brief; the thin depth may explain why it placed below the two that act.

**Medkit (A).** A browser ER and clinic training simulator: the trainee talks to AI patients by voice, orders tests and treats them, and "An attending physician (Claude Opus 4.7) watches and grades your decisions" ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bedriyan/medkit-app/main/README.md)). **Wrench Board (A, post-event history).** "Agent-native diagnostic workbench for board-level electronics repair": the agent builds an electrical graph from schematics and boardviews, picks test points, reads the measurements back and narrows to a diagnosis. README: Maieutic; What it's for; three numbered sections (skills students build, what instructors see, classroom scale); Quick start; Stack; What Opus does; Author (demo and test sections were cut on Apr 24). **ARIA (A).** A maintenance engineer uploads a manufacturer PDF and answers four questions; within about 15 minutes the plant is profiled, and five agents (KB Builder, Sentinel, Investigator, Work Order Generator, Q&A) watch live signals and write work orders. **MaestrIA (C).** From the post only: users photograph a home repair problem, describe it and share a location; Claude streams its reasoning over the photos and returns a structured diagnosis (what is broken, material, severity, budget, time), then a map of nearby craftsmen and a drafted WhatsApp message. **Tekton (A).** "Give Tekton a historical building and Claude researches it, pulling together schematics, construction documents, photographs, and diagrams, then assembles a 3D model across 339 incremental construction states. When you click any component in the model, Tekton shows where the detail came from and why it was placed there." (post). Its thesis: "nothing renders without a cited source." "Independent verifier sub-agents graded each reconstruction in isolated context windows, and self-correction loops rechecked component placement until all 20 tests passed." (post). The kickoff prompt: "The verifier gates completion. You may NOT stop until npm run goal completes green end-to-end." ([KICKOFF_PROMPT.md](https://github.com/tangxiya-star/Tekton/blob/main/docs/KICKOFF_PROMPT.md)). A [done.rubric.json](https://github.com/tangxiya-star/Tekton/blob/main/done.rubric.json) of 31 criteria maps to the judging weights. The first commits are from the day before the event; the post mentions an earlier prototype. README: thesis; winner banner; live demo; building selector; How the 3D is generated; About; What It Does; Why This Matters; The Current Subject; How We Used Agents; Data And Citations; Build Pipeline; Verifier (with the corrupt and restore demo); Run Locally; Tech Stack; Repository Map; Transfer Pattern. Likely reasons: it answered each criterion on purpose (heritage lost to fire, with Notre-Dame as the hook; the verifier refusing a corrupted roof live in the demo; failure reports kept as proof of autonomy; a rubric file for orchestration).

**Sim Francisco (A).** "A working model of San Francisco's population. It has 10,000 synthetic residents drawn from US Census data" that react to the news; it "forecast the 2024 presidential vote at 81.3% Democratic against an actual 83.8%, and San Francisco's March 2024 Prop A at 70% against an actual 70.38%." (post). Before any milestone is done, "an independent agent re-runs `validate` + contract tests, and a second adversarial agent tries to prove the gain is spurious"; the critic caught a prompt line that leaked the 2024 result ([NOTES.md](https://github.com/tejasprabhune/simfrancisco/blob/main/NOTES.md)). Claude also grouped residents into about 300 personas, "cutting inference cost by 10 to 100 times" (post). README: tagline; live links; The brief; What it is; Cost control; Reproduce it; "The four loops (autonomy + orchestration)"; "Repeatable on a new problem (Orchestration criterion)"; "Methodology integrity (no leakage / no gaming)". Likely reasons: a number a judge can check against the real world, an adversarial agent aimed at its own score, and README sections named after the criteria.

**Custom Universe (A).** "Snap a phone photo of a chair, and Custom Universe turns it into a 3D object you can drop into a scene, restyle with a text prompt, and move around while the rendered image updates in real time," for robotics labs that need synthetic training data (post). Opus 4.8 built it and ran the GPU machine; a written retrospective notes "An adversarial multi-agent review of the diff caught a camera-leak/abort-storm". README: purely technical (what it is, requirements, Docker, quick start, how realtime works, model hosting, environment, TRELLIS sidecar, API, next steps), with no problem statement. One commit predates the event (Feb 2026). Likely reasons: the most visual live demo of the three and a clear user; the least written orchestration (no rubric file, no tests), which fits third place.

**Project Blackbox (B).** Alex's own Grand Prize entry, summarised only in the notes: an evidence-gated data incident agent. Claude investigates DataHub's context graph, cannot confirm a root cause without lineage plus numeric evidence, and waits for an operator to press "Repair & Verify"; it then patches the SQL, passes 32 of 32 pipeline invariants, opens a real pull request and writes the incident resolution back to DataHub (51 commits, 58 unit tests, 11 machine-checked eval criteria, two upstream DataHub pull requests). README structure, video and story are not summarised in the notes. The winners post is blocked, so its stated reasons are unknown; it shares every trait the notes found across the four known winners: write-back, code deciding what is true, a human approval before repair, honesty sections, a "For judges" map, and upstream contributions.

**Hindsight (B).** "Your dashboard has been wrong since 03:00. Hindsight walks your DataHub lineage to find what broke it, ranks who it hits, and writes the answer back into the catalog - then re-reads every change through a different API to prove it landed." It saves each postmortem as a DataHub document and recalls it at the next incident; [AGENTS.md](https://github.com/gmassello/hindsight/blob/main/AGENTS.md) calls it "a deterministic phase state machine with an LLM inside each phase, not a free ReAct loop". It proposed an upstream skill ([datahub-skills#110](https://github.com/datahub-project/datahub-skills/pull/110)). README: "Data on-call, not ML drift"; "An agent that reports its own success proves nothing"; Start here; What it actually leaves behind; The 60-second run; How it works; "The memory lives in the catalog, not in a side database"; What gets written, and who inherits it; "Not what DataHub already gives you"; The workflow as a portable Skill; "Every claim, and where to check it"; Honest limits; Tests; Deeper; License. Its SUBMISSION.md maps each criterion to evidence and says the video "Opens with the real on-call pain (first 20 seconds → applicability criterion)". Likely reasons: deep two-way use of DataHub with memory inside the catalog, an approval gate on every write, and an upstream contribution for the bonus.

**Paracelsus (B).** "Risk assessment for your data catalog. Ranks findings by how much harm can actually reach someone, not by how broken something looks." Risk = Hazard x Exposure x Receptor-sensitivity x Uncertainty-factor, borrowed from toxicology; "The score is computed in plain Python, deterministically. No number in the output comes from a language model." Results are written back as structured properties and tags. README: Prerequisites; Quickstart; How the demo numbers reproduce; "Honesty boundary: what's real, what's curated"; Troubleshooting; Known fragility; License. Built Aug 8 to 10, 2026. Likely reasons: originality (a tested method from drug safety in place of naive blast radius), reproducible numbers, and honesty about the seeded defects.

**Culprit (B).** "A stack trace for model decay. It names the column, prices the damage, and opens the PR." Built on a real change in the NYC taxi data: "$90,322 of attributable model error in a single month, across 66,146 real NYC taxi trips, caused by one upstream column's maximum value changing from 6 to 7". It patches the transformation, runs `dbt build` against the real warehouse, and opens a PR only if the fix holds; its first attempt deleted all rows of the new vendor, and "The row-count gate caught it and refused to open the PR". README: 24 sections, opening with "The number" and "Then it wrote the fix, and rejected its own first attempt", then a no-setup path, "For judges: where each criterion is answered", the problem, primary sources, "What is real", "Limitations" and "Design note: the model is the engine". Commit messages show a "truth pass" that corrected six claims before submission. Likely reasons: a dollar figure on real data, a fix that had to prove itself, a visible self-rejection, and a README that answers each criterion by name.
